UX CASE STUDY

Zomato: Reducing Friction in the Food Ordering Journey

Heuristic evaluation · User journey analysis · Three design improvements

1. Overview

Product: Zomato mobile app (food delivery), one of India's most widely used food ordering apps. It lets users discover restaurants, order for delivery, track orders, and pay in-app.

Why Zomato? It is a high-frequency, time-pressured product: users are hungry, often on mobile, and expect an order to take a few taps. Small friction points therefore have a large effect on satisfaction and conversion.

Goal of this study

Map the core ordering journey, identify what works and what frustrates users, and propose three focused improvements with mockups.

2. Methodology

MethodHow it was applied
Task walkthroughStep-by-step review of: find a dinner option → customise → pay → track → receive.
Heuristic evaluationNielsen's 10 usability heuristics, with emphasis on visibility of system status, user control, and error prevention.
PersonaBelow. Used to judge priorities.
Persona: Ananya, 26, software engineer, Hyderabad. Orders dinner 3–4 times a week after work. Wants a decent meal fast, under a budget of about ₹300. Compares 2–3 options, checks delivery time and total price, and gets annoyed by surprises at checkout.

Note on scope: this is a desktop-style heuristic review based on typical app behaviour, not primary research with participants. Features and fees change often, so verify specifics in the live app before citing them.

Emotional curve

Excitement is high when browsing, dips during comparison fatigue, drops at the checkout total if fees appear that were not visible earlier, and recovers when live tracking shows progress. A delayed order with no clear next step is the lowest point.

5. Proposed Improvements

Sketches are low-fidelity. Red dashed outlines mark the current problem; green outlines mark the proposed change.

Improvement 1: Show the "all-in" price early

Problem: Users compare restaurants on dish price and delivery time, then see extra charges only at the cart.
Solution: Add an estimated "typical order total" line on restaurant cards and a fee breakdown tooltip. Show the cart total preview on the sticky cart bar.
Expected impact: fewer checkout surprises, higher trust, lower cart abandonment.

Improvement 2: Remember my filters and "Decide for me"

Problem: Re-setting veg, budget and time filters every session wastes effort and adds to choice overload.
Solution: Save filters as a persistent "My preferences" bar, plus a "Top 3 for you" shortlist based on those filters and past orders.
Expected impact: faster time to first add-to-cart, less scrolling.

Improvement 3: Proactive delay handling on the tracking screen

Problem: When an order runs late, the user must dig through help menus to decide what to do.
Solution: If the estimate slips, show a clear, honest banner with options in context: keep waiting (with an updated time), contact the rider, or get help, with the outcome of each choice explained.
Expected impact: reduced anxiety and support contacts, improved recovery from a bad moment (heuristics 3, 9 and 10).

7. Success Metrics and Next Steps

Measure: cart abandonment rate, time from open to first add-to-cart, share of delayed orders that contact support, and post-order satisfaction (CSAT).

Next: A/B test the price preview on a subset of users, run 5–8 usability tests with frequent orderers, then iterate on fee-breakdown wording.

8. Conclusion

Zomato has a strong core experience: appetising discovery and reassuring live tracking. The biggest opportunities are in price transparency, reducing decision fatigue, and recovering gracefully from delays. Each proposal addresses a moment where a hungry, time-pressed user's trust is most at risk.

Independent academic case study. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Zomato. Observations are based on general app behaviour and should be re-verified against the current version.
